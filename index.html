ABHA 2031

Reimagining India’s Connected Healthcare Ecosystem

“Your health history should follow you, not remain scattered across the hospitals you visit.”

A design research and speculative ecosystem project. It starts from the 2026 baseline of India’s digital health infrastructure and imagines how patient-centred interventions could make health information more accessible, interoperable and trustworthy by 2031.

Overview

Sector: Indian digital healthcare ecosystem.
Baseline: 2026. Horizon: 2031.
Discipline: Interaction Design.
Perspective: Patient-centred.

The Record-to-Care Gap

Having an ABHA identity does not guarantee that a patient’s records are available, interoperable or usable at the point of care. A patient’s digital health information does not automatically become usable clinical information at the point of care. Patients may still carry reports, repeat their history or wait for retrieval across providers. This is a research hypothesis about implementation and patient experience, not a claim that ABDM’s architecture fails.

Research question

How might India’s digital healthcare ecosystem evolve to make healthcare information more accessible, interoperable, trustworthy and patient-centred by 2031?

Existing versus proposed. ABHA is a digital health identity, not health insurance. ABDM enables consent-based exchange; it is not a central repository, and records stay with the providers that created them. Sharing depends on consent, authorisation and law, and insurers do not automatically receive ABHA-linked records. HealthGraph, ConsentLens, DataRights Ledger and all other named innovations are speculative design proposals, not official ABDM products or deployed services.

3.6 Validation and impact
No results yet. Everything below is a proposed outcome or plan. No usability, adoption or impact figures have been measured.
2026 problem2031 proposed intervention

Hard-to-read consentConsentLens

Proposed benefits: patients gain control and fewer repeats; doctors see relevant history sooner; hospitals reduce record chasing; laboratories gain traceable delivery; caregivers get delegated, bounded access.

Usability testing plan (not yet run), five tasks: find a previous diagnostic report in HealthGraph; identify a record’s source; review and approve or decline a request in ConsentLens; find which organisation accessed the records; report an unfamiliar access event in the Ledger.

Evaluation metrics: task completion rate, time per task, navigation errors, understanding of consent, ability to identify data recipients, participant confidence and feedback.

Limitations: depends on provider participation and data quality; needs secure, reliable interoperability; consent workflows must align with governance and law; offline and multilingual features need testing; any AI summaries must stay traceable to original records and subject to clinical review.

A new design direction, and how the system works

ABHA 2031 is a proposed patient-centred, distributed ecosystem: identity connects the records, providers keep their original records, patients keep access to their own history, and every exchange is authorised and accountable.

Five principles

  1. ABHA-linked identity is the reference for record matching (names are shown, never used as the matching key).
  2. Distributed storage, not one central repository.
  3. The patient is primary custodian of their accumulated history.
  4. Providers keep original records and answer for their authenticity and retention.
  5. Sharing stays on the existing consent-based exchange.

From provider-centric to patient-centric

Today the record follows the hospital. In this proposal a verified copy follows the patient, while the provider’s original stays put. Identity verification, authentication, record discovery, consent and transmission remain separate operations. An ABHA number alone never opens every provider’s records.

Medical document lifecycle (Ananya Sharma, fictional)

Custody and retention (proposed). The provider keeps the original during and after treatment. The patient holds a durable personal copy. A one-month post-treatment milestone is for copy handover and reconciliation only: it is not deletion. Providers follow all legal and professional retention obligations, and the patient keeps continuing access.

Patient upload workflow

  1. Patient selects a document (PDF, photo, scan, older report, discharge summary).
  2. It uploads to secure personal health storage.
  3. OCR and AI suggest the document type.
  4. Date, provider, test or medicine and details are extracted.
  5. Patient reviews the extracted information.
  6. System checks for gaps and possible duplicates.
  7. The original file is preserved.
  8. Labelled patient uploaded and unverified unless independently authenticated.
  9. Patient confirms the metadata.
  10. It appears in HealthGraph.

AI assists with extraction and organisation. It does not authenticate clinical documents or make medical decisions. Try it in the live prototype.

Privacy and trust

Encryption

At rest and in transit; protected key management; secure key recovery.

Identity and access

ABHA-linked identity, strong authentication, provider and professional verification, role-based access, zero-trust checks.

Consent and minimisation

Named requester, purpose, categories, duration and scope; approve or decline; expiry and revocation where applicable.

Accountability

Access logs with organisation, purpose, time and categories; patient-facing history; suspicious-activity alerts; report a concern. A log supports investigation; it does not prove wrongdoing.

Resilience

Encrypted backups, recovery, ransomware protection, incident response.

Future options to investigate

Federated learning, differential privacy, secure multiparty computation, confidential computing, verifiable credentials, post-quantum cryptography. Blockchain is not assumed.
Emergency access (concept). Only a narrowly scoped, time-limited, logged and reviewable workflow, never unrestricted access. Exact rules would need legal and clinical governance. See the Contextual Integrity audit in Sprint 03.

    Reflection

    The project moves from understanding the 2026 ecosystem to speculating a 2031 one, then to designing the experience a patient would touch. The strongest lesson is that trust is designed through small moments: understanding a request, seeing the source of a record, and seeing who looked.

    Open opportunities: usability testing, assisted-access research with healthcare workers and legal review of the proposed consent patterns.
